6.1 Let Agent do the heavy lift#

From individual skills to complete tasks and changing environments, the preceding sections established a way to design and validate robot behavior. First use an agent to understand the course and troubleshoot labs, then apply the same approach to your own robot task.

Section 6.1 · Course help and your own robot task

First, use your agent as a course teaching assistant#

Give your agent ROBOT_TASK_GUIDE.en.md and the course repository when a concept is unclear or a lab fails. The guide includes a teaching-assistant mode: answer only questions related to this course and the environment needed for its labs. For unrelated questions, the agent should explain its scope and redirect to the course.

Ask it to compare imitation and reinforcement learning, explain the arrows and time axes in a control-cycle diagram, or break a concept into smaller steps and examples. It can also inspect the relevant lesson, code and logs when container builds, Isaac startup, training or shooting commands fail. If it cannot access your computer, provide the section, command, complete error and relevant device information. You do not need the full robot-task brief below just to ask a course question.

Start with this prompt:

Read ROBOT_TASK_GUIDE.en.md and act as my teaching assistant for the Microduck course. Answer only questions related to the course and its required lab environment. I am studying [section] and my question is [fill in]. Read the relevant course pages and code, then explain concisely, interpret the diagram or give a step-by-step example, citing your sources. For a runtime problem, inspect the command, environment and error first, then propose the smallest fix and a verification step. Ask for missing information when needed; do not guess the cause or claim a run succeeded without evidence.

The guide defines a scope and troubleshooting approach; it does not replace course materials or execution evidence. Ask the agent to identify the section or code supporting its answer. After a fix, rerun the failing step to confirm the issue is resolved. You can then apply the same approach to designing your own robot task.

Bring this task guide#

ROBOT_TASK_GUIDE.en.md, the single English guide in the repository, turns the course's workflow and lessons into a project guide that you can give directly to an agent. It does not depend on a particular tool or require a shooting task. Microduck can reuse the course assets and skills; another robot requires its own joint, action and physics checks.

Provide the English guide with your project; you can discuss the task with your agent in your preferred language. Ask it to inspect the model and sensors, ask for necessary missing information, recommend models and whether training is needed, plan data collection, and then propose staged implementation. It does not need to be named AGENTS.md. If your tool reads that project convention, merge the relevant instructions into your own AGENTS.md.

Supply the essential context#

Information

What to specify

Microduck shooting example

Robot

USD, URDF or MJCF, meshes, units, joint names/order, limits and default pose

14 controlled joints with a fixed action mapping

Simulation

Isaac Sim / Isaac Lab versions, physics backend, ground, obstacles and contacts

Newton, flat pitch, ball and goal

Task and acceptance

Starts, goal, allowed actions, completion, timeout and failure definitions

Approach, actual foot-ball contact, whole ball across the goal and standing recovery

Observations

Cameras, ToF, IMU and joints, with rates, frames and latency

Visual target estimates and body feedback

Existing capabilities

Reusable models, input/output contracts, data and runnable entry points

Walking, kicking and standing recovery

Resources

Hardware, memory, acceptable runtime, training budget and output location

Small initial checks and separate output directories

Write “unknown; inspect or propose a measurement” when information is missing. Do not let the agent guess joint order, units or success criteria: a program may run while producing meaningless results.

The guide asks the agent to distinguish the sensors actually available from the information the task needs: what can be read directly, what must be estimated, and what is missing. Given existing skills, resources and data, it should explain whether to reuse rules or models, or train perception, reinforcement-learning policies or a VLA. If training is needed, it should specify data sources, collection, annotation and independent validation splits.

Advance through verifiable steps#

The shooting project illustrates how to locate an error before choosing a change. Incorrect ball position calls for sensor, coordinate and timing checks. Drifting despite seeing the ball calls for command, feedback and braking checks. A missed kick from a suitable stance calls for contact-geometry and skill-handoff checks. Falling loss, smooth video and one success do not replace task acceptance.

Give the agent a concrete starting point#

Adapt this brief and attach the model, scene and guide:

Use ROBOT_TASK_GUIDE.en.md to help implement a simulated robot task. The robot and model paths are [fill in], simulation versions and physics backend are [fill in], and available sensors and policies are [fill in]. In [scene and starting range], it should complete [task]. Success requires [measurable conditions]; failures include [fill in]. Resources and budget are [fill in]. First inspect the files and interfaces, ask about available and task-required sensor inputs, and recommend models, whether training is needed and a data-collection plan, explaining the rationale and unresolved decisions. Then begin with the smallest verifiable closed loop, provide actual evidence at each stage, preserve failures and original models, and advance only when the stage is ready.

On resuming work, ask the agent to read the saved configuration, results and unresolved questions. This gives the next session a concrete starting point and makes the remaining work visible.

Sources and usage terms#

Robot geometry, community policies, and dependencies each follow their own source and license terms; for third-party assets those terms apply, and the course-code license does not replace them. Before redistributing changes, read the repository’s attribution and license notices.

With your task brief ready, use the exercises and official resources in Section 6.2 to check configurations, understand the methods and choose a well-defined experiment.
