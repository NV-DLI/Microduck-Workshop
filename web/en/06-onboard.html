4.2 Designing the shooting stages#

Section 4.1 turned sensor readings into ball and goal positions. Use them to find the targets, approach the ball, align for a kick and return to standing.

Section 4.2 · Estimated learning time: 20 minutes

Why an onboard policy#

Section 2.2 introduced Microduck's RK3566 controller and its 0.8 TOPS NPU. This compute is limited for low-latency inference of large vision-language-action models (VLAs) such as GR00T. Here, a small detector, geometry and feedback control call trained walking and kicking skills. The experiment runs these functions in Isaac; the complete program has not been validated on RK3566.

From sensors to motion#

RGB detection and ToF ranging provide position estimates. High-level control requests forward, sideways and turning velocities, while the walking policy handles balance and stepping. Isaac Lab organizes observations and control; Newton computes movement and contact.

Four steps of a shot#

Work backward from the kick conditions: the ball must be in front of the right foot, the body must face the goal, and the robot must stand still. These four teaching steps group the task by purpose; the program retains its observation, braking and handoff checks.

1. Find targets#

Turn the head to find the goal and ball, confirming positions across observations. Observe again when information becomes stale.

2. Straight approach#

On this obstacle-free pitch, follow a straight segment checked for clearance toward the stance behind the ball. PD feedback corrects heading, lateral position error adjusts sideways motion, and forward speed decreases with distance.

3. Close-range alignment#

Place the ball in front of the right foot while facing the goal. Continue correcting and brake early. If ball position or heading is unreliable, stop to observe again; when needed, retreat only after checking rear clearance, then approach again. Losing sight of the ball alone never triggers a kick.

4. Kick and recover#

Stop, return the head, and confirm 0.5 seconds of continuous stillness plus arrival checks before handing control to the official right-kick policy. Blend back to the walking policy with zero velocity to recover standing. A separate simulation evaluator judges whether the ball entered the goal.

Connecting the four steps to execution#

Observations and body state drive transitions, with returns to observation or alignment when needed. This real excerpt shows where the modules meet on each control update: last_control supplies finding and approach requests, fine_state supplies close-range correction, and handoff_state controls stopping and kick authorization. The code's written order is not the temporal order of the four steps.

What was trained and what was not#

  • Detector. Its goal is to find two classes, ball and goal, in 960 × 540 head-camera images. The course started from a YOLO26n detector and trained it further on pitch images rendered in Isaac Sim, covering several floors (including the course's green pitch), different camera poses and ball positions. Detection-box labels were generated automatically by projecting the known scene geometry into the camera image, with rendered depth removing occluded parts. 512 images were used for training, 128 to select the checkpoint and another 128 held out for testing.

  • Walking. The official walking policy was trained by Pollen Robotics with reinforcement learning, the method of Part 3; the course uses it with unchanged weights.

  • Kick and recovery. The official right-foot kick policy is also a local skill trained with reinforcement learning, with unchanged weights; the timing logic for switching between it and standing is implemented here.

  • Not trained. Geometry, straight-line generation, velocity feedback, braking and stage logic were programmed.

Perception, continuous feedback and skill handover now form one controller. In Section 4.3, run the complete shooting experiment and observe correction, braking and the kick working together.
