4.3 Goooal!#

Put the control design from Section 4.2 onto the pitch. Observe how sensing, stage transitions and low-level skills work together, then use the records from each round to locate failures.

Section 4.3 · Estimated time 20 minutes

Goooal!#

Prepare the course container as described in Section 1.2, then return to its terminal. If it is already running, enter it from the host with docker exec -it microduck-workshop bash. Run:

Terminal · Inside the container#
python3 scripts/run.py football --output outputs/onboard-football
Parameter details

  • --output: A new folder; rounds are kept in separate episode-001, episode-002, etc. folders

The robot automatically observes, approaches the ball and attempts a shot. The first round starts from the nominal position. Click the Isaac Sim window and press R to restart: the window stays open and each round starts from the standing initialization, with the start randomly offset by up to 5 cm forward/backward and up to 5 cm sideways, and the heading turned by up to 15° clockwise or counterclockwise. The ball and goal stay in place. Allow a moment for the scene to initialize; the task then starts automatically.

The student experiment has no overall time limit. Scoring, missing or being unable to kick does not automatically pause the simulation; sensors keep updating. Press R to reset the attempt. Close Isaac or press Ctrl+C in its terminal to stop the program. Perception, alignment or handover failures pause the current maneuver and trigger another observation attempt.

The simulation renders several views at once, the third-person viewport, the head RGB camera and ToF, so its speed depends on your hardware and the motion in the window may look jerky; the task advances on simulation time.

summary.json summarizes saved rounds. Each round's manifest.json includes navigation_retries, which records why the robot reacquired observations. A visible detection box does not mean the geometry has passed its checks; if the close-range position is not fresh enough, the policy pauses to observe again. Also check lateral error and heading during the approach, whether the kick conditions were met and whether the robot recovered its balance after contact.

When you press R, the program saves the current record before starting the next round.

The onboard control cycle#

Here, motion requests specify forward, lateral and turning motion. The trained walking Actor takes these requests together with body feedback and produces joint targets; the implementation does not first create foot trajectories and then solve IK. Joint drives continue to track the target angles. Section 2.2 distinguishes this learned control path from inverse kinematics and inverse dynamics.

Control cycle

Onboard policy: RGB and ToF use nominal 15 Hz captures, followed by YOLO inference and localization. Every 20 ms, feedback control uses the latest perception and fresh body feedback to generate a motion request; the low-level Actor then generates joint targets.

Rates are simulation settings, not measured runtime speed. Simulation pauses during computation; host inference time and simulation time use separate clocks and are not added.

Limits of hand design#

In the experiment, heading PD, lateral correction and trained skills produce motion; stage logic decides when to approach, stop and kick. These roles make the controller easier to analyze and debug. For "dribble the ball to the center circle and stop" or "walk up to the ball without touching it", perception and feedback control remain reusable, but target positions, skill combinations, transitions and parameters need new design and validation.

If model inference can move off the robot to a GPU workstation, or to a cloud server at the cost of more network delay, a much larger VLA becomes possible: one that learns from a language instruction and demonstrations instead of hand-written stages, while trained motion skills still handle fast control.
