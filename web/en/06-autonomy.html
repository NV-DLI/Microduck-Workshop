SELF-PACED WORKSHOP

From engineered pipelines to intelligent robots

See how the engineered football pipeline works, then identify what learned policies and reasoning agents could take over.

Chapter 6 · Estimated learning time: 15 minutes

One field, two ways to score

Start with one simple indoor field: one football mat, background, 10 cm ball and goal. Walking uses a leg kick; skating uses controlled contact. Both must send the whole ball through the goal and finish stably. This chapter requires only observation and reflection.

Projection, coordinate transforms and a geometric offset behind the ball turn observations into movement goals. Understanding their purpose is enough here. The walking example uses Nav2/Navfn paths and traversability checks, followed by a custom vision-guided stage controller. Skating uses visual feedback without Nav2. Nav2 is an open ROS 2 navigation framework that can integrate with Isaac ROS perception components. The demonstration uses the Nav2 path.

Watch the walking shot

Notice the switches between looking down and adjusting heading. Turning changes the ball position relative to the robot, requiring fresh observations. Residual motion can trigger another alignment cycle. Useful individual skills still need coordinated observation, braking and transitions.

Watch the gliding shot

Skating must give the ball enough speed while stopping the robot in time. Velocity feedback controls braking, with skating active throughout. This demonstration estimates speed by integrating an ideal IMU; real sensor bias and delay require calibration. Compare the videos: a shared goal requires different actions and final conditions.

How to judge reliable performance

A successful video demonstrates possibility. Reliability needs repeated trials excluded from tuning. On the same field, vary the start, ball position and initial heading modestly. Evaluate walking and skating separately using complete success, falls, repeated observations and time. A goal followed by failed stopping is a separate outcome.

Ask in order: was the target seen, was its position estimated accurately, did the action reach its goal, and did the robot finish stably? Accurate detection alone cannot guarantee the later stages. A successful demonstration does not imply success on every trial.

Let demonstrations and experience do more

Explicit pipelines are understandable and diagnosable, but people must design goals, stages and transitions. Moving the ball elsewhere or tackling a longer task requires new coordination. Could instructions and demonstrations teach the actions, while an agent organizes those abilities?

Next, GR00T explains learning actions from observations; ASPIRE explains organizing and improving skills from execution feedback. These are method-design lessons for Microduck. Focus on the required data, the model’s control role and how to evaluate results. Expand environments after the methods work reliably on one field.
