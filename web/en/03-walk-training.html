3.3 Train and test a walking policy#

The observations, actions, rewards and Actor-Critic roles are now defined. Put them into practice: examine domain randomization, read the reward and PPO code, start parallel training and test an exported walking policy.

Section 3.3 · Hands-on · Estimated learning time: 45 minutes (watching training for a few minutes is enough; a full run takes about 2 hours and is optional)

What domain randomization is#

Every simulation parameter is one definite number: foot friction, torso mass, the delay between a servo receiving a target and starting to move… Real robots all differ. Domain randomization takes a simple approach: since we do not know the real values, each parallel environment draws its own set of parameters during training. The policy faces thousands of “slightly different robots” at once and earns a high overall return only by learning actions that work for all of them.

Learning objective under domain randomization
\[\htmlData{sym=J_dr}{J(\htmlData{sym=theta}{\theta})}=\htmlData{sym=E_xi}{\mathbb E_{\xi\sim p(\xi)}}\,\htmlData{sym=E_traj}{\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta,\,\xi}}\!\left[\sum_{\htmlData{sym=t_idx}{t}=0}^{\htmlData{sym=T_horizon}{T}-1}\htmlData{sym=gamma}{\gamma}^{\htmlData{sym=t_idx}{t}}\htmlData{sym=r_t}{r_t}\right]\]
Variable meanings
\(J(\theta)\)
Learning objective under domain randomization: the expected discounted return averaged over many random sets of simulation parameters.
\(\theta\)
Policy network parameters (weights), updated during training.
\(\mathbb E_{\xi\sim p(\xi)}\)
Average over randomly drawn parameter sets.
\(\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta,\,\xi}\)
Average over trajectories sampled with the current policy in the simulation defined by \(\xi\).
\(t\)
Control-step index.
\(T\)
Number of control steps of an episode that count toward the return.
\(\gamma\)
Discount factor; 0.99 in the course training.
\(r_t\)
Immediate reward at the current control step: the weighted sum of the reward terms.

Compared with Section 3.1 there is one more average: first draw a parameter set \(\htmlData{sym=xi}{\xi}\) from the distribution \(\htmlData{sym=p_xi}{p(\xi)}\), then sample trajectories with policy \(\htmlData{sym=pi_theta}{\pi_\theta}\) under those parameters. The PPO update is unchanged; what changes is the data, since one batch mixes many conditions.

Why walking needs it#

Walking depends entirely on foot-ground contact and servo response, which are exactly what simulation finds hardest to get right: servo friction differs between batches, battery charge changes servo output, encoder zeros are slightly off, and floors are soft or hard. A policy trained only on one “perfect” parameter set may rely on its details and lose balance on another robot or floor. This gap between simulation and reality is often called the sim-to-real gap.

Choosing ranges is a trade-off. Too narrow, and the policy still fits only a few cases; too wide, and the task may become too hard, so the policy learns conservative, sluggish motions or nothing at all. Ranges are best based on measurements or hardware specifications, such as measured servo delays or the battery's operating voltage. Domain randomization covers only the differences you thought of and wrote into the configuration; it does not replace testing on hardware.

Which randomizations this training uses, and how they are configured#

Isaac Lab implements randomization with its event manager. Each event has a trigger: startup samples once when the environment is created and then keeps the value; reset samples again whenever that environment resets; interval fires at time intervals during an episode. The table comes from the env.yaml saved by the full training. Note that it stores both initial values and curriculum settings: some ranges widen as training progresses, so the initial fields alone are not the whole story.

1Foot friction is sampled when environments are created

mode="startup" draws once per environment. Static and dynamic friction lie in 0.7–1.3; num_buckets splits the range into 64 levels so thousands of environments share a few materials.

1
foot_friction = EventTerm(
    func=mdp.randomize_rigid_body_material,
    mode="startup",
    params={
        "asset_cfg": SceneEntityCfg("robot", body_names=MICRODUCK_FOOT_BODY_NAMES),
        "static_friction_range": (0.7, 1.3),
        "dynamic_friction_range": (0.7, 1.3),
        "restitution_range": (0.0, 0.0),
        "num_buckets": 64,
    },
)

2Encoder zero offset

Each joint reading gets a fixed offset within ±0.015 rad, imitating assembly and calibration error; the joint angles the policy sees differ slightly from the true ones.

2
encoder_bias = EventTerm(func=mdp.randomize_encoder_bias, mode="startup", params={"bias_range": (-0.015, 0.015)})

3New joint friction at every reset

mode="reset" gives an environment a new friction set every time it restarts, so even one environment experiences different servo behaviour during training.

3
randomize_joint_friction = EventTerm(
    func=mdp.randomize_bam_friction, mode="reset", params={"scale_range": (0.9, 1.1)}
)

4A random push while walking

The interval event changes the body's horizontal velocity by up to 0.3 m/s every 3–6 seconds; the policy has to learn to recover its balance.

4
push_robot = EventTerm(
    func=mdp.push_by_setting_velocity,
    mode="interval",
    interval_range_s=(3.0, 6.0),
    params={"velocity_range": {"x": (-0.3, 0.3), "y": (-0.3, 0.3)}},
)

The excerpt comes from the Microduck task configuration of the pinned Isaac Lab. Servo delay and supply voltage are not events but part of the actuator model; observation noise is part of the observation configuration. The test later in this section turns pushes and parameter randomization off to compare policies under nominal conditions.

Pollen Robotics also uses domain randomization to train the official policies. Its training repository, microduck_rl, is built on mjlab rather than this course's Isaac Lab and Newton, so the two are not the same configuration; its walking task likewise randomizes foot friction (also 0.7–1.3), mass and inertia, trunk and head center of mass, joint friction and armature, encoder zero offsets and IMU mounting angle, and applies periodic pushes (official walking task configuration ↗). The official README adds that its servo model randomizes supply voltage, voltage sag, command delay and friction (microduck_rl ↗).

Core implementation of the reward and PPO#

Section 3.2 described which terms make up the reward. Take the linear-velocity tracking term as an example of how it is written in code:

Linear-velocity tracking reward and total reward
\[\htmlData{sym=r_v}{r_t^{v}}=\htmlData{sym=exp}{\exp}\!\left(-\frac{\|\htmlData{sym=v_cmd}{\mathbf v_{xy}^{\mathrm{cmd}}}-\htmlData{sym=v_xy}{\mathbf v_{xy}}\|_2^2}{\htmlData{sym=sigma_v}{\sigma_v}^2}\right),\qquad \htmlData{sym=r_t}{r_t}=\htmlData{sym=dt}{\Delta t}\sum_{\htmlData{sym=i}{i}}\htmlData{sym=w_i}{w_i}\htmlData{sym=r_it}{r_{i,t}}\]
Variable meanings
\(r_t^{v}\)
Linear-velocity tracking reward at the current control step, between 0 and 1.
\(\exp\)
Exponential: 1 at zero error, approaching 0 as the error grows.
\(\mathbf v_{xy}^{\mathrm{cmd}}\)
Horizontal components of the velocity command (m/s).
\(\mathbf v_{xy}\)
Actual horizontal body velocity (m/s).
\(\sigma_v\)
Tolerance scale of the velocity error, `std` in the code.
\(r_t\)
Immediate reward at the current control step: the weighted sum of the reward terms.
\(\Delta t\)
Duration of one control step, 0.02 s: reward terms are accumulated per time step.
\(i\)
Index of a reward term.
\(w_i\)
Weight of a reward term.
\(r_{i,t}\)
Value of a reward term at the current control step.

1Compare velocity in the horizontal heading frame

Rotate world velocity using the body's yaw so that pitching does not redefine forward.

1
actual = math_utils.quat_apply_inverse(
    math_utils.yaw_quat(asset.data.root_link_quat_w.torch), asset.data.root_link_lin_vel_w.torch
)

2Convert velocity error into reward

Smaller horizontal error gives a reward closer to 1. The tolerance std is 0.15 m/s and this term has weight 4; other terms constrain balance.

2
error = torch.sum(torch.square(command[:, :2] - actual[:, :2]), dim=1)
return zero_non_finite(torch.exp(-error / std**2))

PPO uses the RSL-RL 5.5.1 implementation. This is the clipped objective of Section 3.1:

Python · Core code excerptFull source ↗

1Ratio of new to old policy

Subtracting log probabilities and exponentiating gives the ratio for the same action under the new and old policy. The old log probability is stored at sampling time.

1
ratio = torch.exp(
    actions_log_prob - torch.squeeze(batch.old_actions_log_prob)
)

2Clip

Advantage times ratio gives the surrogate objective; clip_param is the clipping threshold 0.2.

2
surrogate = -torch.squeeze(batch.advantages) * ratio
surrogate_clipped = -torch.squeeze(batch.advantages) * torch.clamp(
    ratio, 1.0 - self.clip_param, 1.0 + self.clip_param
)

3Aggregate the loss

The optimizer minimizes, so the code negates the objective and takes the larger term, equivalent to “maximize the smaller term”.

3
surrogate_loss = torch.max(surrogate, surrogate_clipped).mean()
Parameter details

  • Discount factor, GAE parameter: 0.99, 0.95

  • Clipping threshold: 0.2

  • Steps per environment per iteration: 24

  • Epochs per batch, mini-batches: 5, 4

  • Learning rate: 0.001, adapted by KL divergence

  • Value-loss coefficient, entropy coefficient: 1.0, 0.01

Start a full training and watch it in parallel#

Run the command below in the course container shell from Section 1.2 (to reopen it, see Section 1.2). It trains from a random initialization and reads no weights from any existing model; --show opens the Isaac Sim window so you see a thousand ducks practising at once, each in its own randomized environment. Use a new folder for --output.

Terminal · inside the container#
python3 scripts/run.py train-walk \
  --num-envs 4096 \
  --iterations 2500 \
  --show \
  --output outputs/walking-training-01
Parameter details

  • --num-envs 4096: Environments running on the GPU at the same time; each has its own random parameters

  • --iterations 2500: PPO sampling-and-update loops; the matching sample count is computed above

  • --show: Opens Isaac Sim to display the parallel environments; rendering uses GPU memory and slows every iteration

  • --output: Where checkpoints, configuration, TensorBoard logs and the exported model are saved

How long does a full training take? On DGX Spark, 4096 environments for 2500 iterations without a window took 6217 seconds, about 1 hour 44 minutes. Reaching the selected iteration 1001 took about 43 minutes, rather than 10 minutes. The command uses the same training recipe and full-body collision model as the supplied policy; --show adds rendering costs and the machine load affects timing.

The command does not stop automatically after a few minutes. It runs all 2,500 requested iterations unless you stop it yourself during the observation exercise. Once the window appears and the terminal prints reward and episode length for each iteration, watch for a few minutes: in the first tens of iterations most ducks fall quickly and are reset one by one, and as iterations go by more and more stay up.

When you have seen enough, press Ctrl+C; the container keeps running. Checkpoints already saved (one every 250 iterations) remain, but exported/policy.onnx is exported only when training finishes. The test below uses the course's pretrained policy by default; to let your own training finish, you can drop --show to speed it up, then select your own model explicitly.

Training time and how to judge the result#

datasets/walking-straight (about 11.5 MB) contains the selected iteration-1001 checkpoint, its Actor export, configuration and the full 2500-iteration TensorBoard log. Checkpoint filenames are zero-based: model_1000.pt means 1001 completed iterations. The chart shows the complete run; the test uses an intermediate checkpoint selected by independent walking evaluation, rather than assuming the last checkpoint is best.

Read the metric names in the chart (values below are unsmoothed):

  • Mean episode length is about 986 steps at iteration 1001, close to the 1000-step maximum, indicating balance through most episodes.

  • Mean episode reward is about 167 at iteration 1001 and 138 at the end. Reward weights, head commands and disturbances change with the curriculum, so a single total score is not enough to select a policy.

  • Linear-velocity error drops from about 0.36 m/s to about 0.079 m/s at iteration 1001.

  • Yaw-rate error drops from about 1.77 rad/s to about 0.54 rad/s. Independent direction-control tests remain necessary.

Test a candidate from rest, with pushes disabled, walking 1–2 metres at a fixed command. Check falls, starting yaw and lateral displacement. Velocity tracking rewards do not guarantee a perfectly straight world-frame path. Later sections retain the official skills as separate fixed references.

A very short run makes the gap even clearer: after only a few tens of iterations the reward is already rising, yet the exported policy usually falls within a second. A workflow that runs is not a policy that has learned the skill.

Files a training run writes#

Training writes several kinds of files to its output folder, each for a different purpose: model_*.pt are training checkpoints saved every 250 iterations, env.yaml and agent.yaml record the environment and algorithm configuration actually used, and exported/policy.onnx is an inference file containing only the Actor. A checkpoint holds training state such as the optimizer; ONNX keeps only the computation graph needed for inference, so neither replaces the other.

Check your understanding

If only the ONNX file is kept, can training be resumed exactly from it?

Show explanation

No. ONNX contains only the Actor's inference graph; resuming also needs the Critic, optimizer state, normalization state and the same configuration, so keep the full checkpoint and training configuration.

Test the trained policy#

The command below tests the selected iteration-1001 policy on flat ground. --model explicitly selects this policy; other course activities continue to use their official models.

Terminal · inside the container#
python3 scripts/run.py walk \
  --model datasets/walking-straight/exported/policy.onnx \
  --velocity 0.25 0 0 \
  --seconds 8 \
  --seed 202 \
  --output outputs/ppo-walk-test-01
Parameter details

  • --model: The policy file to test; here the course's pretrained datasets/walking-straight/exported/policy.onnx (read-only)

  • --velocity 0.25 0 0: Forward, sideways and yaw-rate commands; applied from the first policy output after grounded initialization, without an extra two-second zero-command hold

  • --seconds 8: Simulated run time

  • --seed 202: Keep this test seed unchanged when comparing policies

  • --output: Folder for report.json and trajectory.json, with one position every 0.1 s

The fixed camera keeps the start and about two metres of travel visible together. Watch the transition from standing to stepping, then compare the actual direction. In two nominal startup tests, the selected policy travelled 10 cm in about 0.5 seconds; at two metres its lateral offset was about 6–7 cm and maximum heading deviation about 5°, with no falls. These short-distance tests do not establish performance at every speed, on slopes or over long distances.

Startup belongs in policy evaluation. First rule out control delays such as a zero-command hold. If difficulty remains, add training examples that start from rest and then receive a forward command. Compare both startup time and heading error so that faster starts do not come at the expense of balance.

Testing your own policy. Only when your training has run to completion and outputs/walking-training-01/exported/policy.onnx exists, change --model in the command to that path by hand and use a new --output folder. One successful run is one sample; to draw conclusions, repeat with several commands and from different starts, and keep the failures.

Check your understanding

Why turn off the random pushes and parameter randomization of training when testing?

Show explanation

This exercise first turns off pushes and parameter randomization to compare basic responses under nominal conditions. To evaluate robustness, define the distributions and test seeds in advance, expose each policy to the same parameter draws and disturbances, and report every result. Randomized evaluation can still be reproducible.

Extention: compare official and course-trained walking#

Does the same forward command produce the same gait? This recording runs two Microducks simultaneously in one Isaac simulation. The upper view uses the Pollen Robotics v8 official walking policy supplied with the course; the lower view uses the selected iteration-1001 policy. Ground, standing initialization, forward command and fixed camera views match. Playback is synchronized by simulation time.

Compare the first step and distance travelled at the same time, then watch foot lift, body motion and head posture. In this run, both stay upright. The course-trained policy starts moving forward sooner and travels farther; the official policy advances more slowly. A velocity command is a target, not a guarantee of identical actual speed. The video reveals differences, but one run cannot establish overall superiority.

Several factors could contribute:

  • Reward and constraint tradeoffs. Velocity tracking, posture, joint limits, torque and action-change penalties jointly shape a gait. Penalizing action changes can encourage smoothness while affecting response. This exercise retains the pinned Isaac Lab official rewards and curricula, without an added head-height lock; the video alone cannot identify one reward as the cause.

  • Training experience. Our recipe assigns 60% of command samples to forward-only motion, uses symmetry augmentation and resets promptly after a fall. Commands, randomization and initialization determine which states the policy encounters. Different experience can produce different gaits even with the same rewards.

  • Checkpoint stage. We selected a checkpoint after 1001 completed iterations. Further training changes the policy and some reward weights. More iterations do not guarantee a better short-distance result. The official weights' complete training iteration count was not verified here, so training duration alone cannot explain the difference.

  • The transition from standing to walking. Applying the target velocity immediately, ramping it up, or switching between standing and walking policies changes the first step. Pollen Robotics' deployment configuration includes exponential smoothing of velocity commands. In this comparison, both receive the full forward command from their first policy output, with no extra wait or ramp for either one. Different startup smoothing therefore does not explain the differences shown here.

If you could change only one factor, would you first compare reward weights, checkpoint iterations or the startup velocity ramp? Hold the other conditions fixed and compare startup time, displacement and stability to investigate the cause.
