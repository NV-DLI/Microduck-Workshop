SELF-PACED WORKSHOP

Navigate to a useful kicking position

Move beyond chasing the ball: approach from behind it and align with the goal.

Chapter 6 · About 30 minutes

The ball center is not the destination

Walking straight toward the ball center can cause premature contact or approach from the wrong side. A useful target lies behind the ball. The ball-to-goal direction defines the shooting axis; move backward along it and account for the kicking foot’s lateral offset.

A planar geometry exercise

Direction d = (goal − ball) / ‖goal − ball‖
Target target = ball − distance × d + lateral × normal(d)

Terminal · Bash
python3 scripts/workshop.py plan \
  --ball 0.00 -0.02 \
  --goal 0.93 0.00 \
  --distance 0.18 \
  --lateral 0.035 \
  --output outputs/plan-01

Expected result: a target position, heading, and a planar diagram. Change the ball’s lateral coordinate and observe how the target moves. This exercise uses known 2D points to teach geometry; autonomous execution uses sensor estimates.

How path planning and walking divide the work

A planner searches for a route through a representation of space. An occupancy grid divides the floor into cells for obstacles, free space, and unknown space. A robot has a footprint: it cannot pass through every gap that admits a mathematical point. Reserving clearance is necessary when turning a geometric route into executable motion.

Nav2 provides CPU navigation services in the full task, while the walking policy maintains bipedal balance. The upper layer requests a target or velocity; the lower layer drives servos. Actuator delay and gait error can move the robot away from its plan, so the system must observe and reconsider instead of assuming exact command execution.

The hand-calculation exercise studies stance geometry only: it does not call Nav2 or place obstacles. The full task uses one robot, ball, and goal in an empty field. Adding obstacles requires new checks for sensor coverage, unknown space, clearance, and collisions. Empty-field results do not establish obstacle-avoidance performance.

Approach in stages

  1. 1Search and observe
  2. 2Coarse approach
  3. 3Fine alignment
  4. 4Check kick conditions
  5. 5Kick and recover

At a distance, reducing range is the main priority. Nearby, motion must become more precise while preserving visibility, heading, and foot placement. Being close enough is not sufficient if heading is wrong.

A planner supplies a route or direction; the walking policy executes it. A valid planned path does not prove the robot has traversed it. The system must handle tracking errors, contact, and sensor updates.

Reobserve when information is lost

Check your understanding

ToF becomes invalid during approach, but the remembered ball position looks plausible. Should the robot continue forward?

Show explanation

Not merely because it looks plausible. Check memory age and current ground, range, and attitude information. Stop and reobserve when the required conditions are absent.

Work through a stance target

Subtract the ball position from the goal center and normalize the result to obtain the shooting direction. The stance lies behind the ball: move back by distance along that direction, then sideways by lateral to allow space for the kicking leg. This exercise defines its own offset convention; it does not replace calibrated entry values in the physical skill.

Open plan.svg to inspect the stance, ball, and goal. plan.json gives the target position and heading. Change distance from 0.18 to 0.25 with a new output directory; predict the direction of movement and check it. This command computes geometry from known points. It runs no physics and does not prove reachability.

The full task also handles yaw drift, delayed gait response, and targets leaving view at close range. Approach therefore updates observations and checks stopping conditions repeatedly. If valid range or floor support is lost, stop or retreat within constraints and observe again. Unknown space cannot be declared free space.
